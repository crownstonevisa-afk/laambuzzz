Differences Between Auto Collision and Semi-Truck Injury Claims

When most people picture a car accident claim, they imagine two drivers, two insurance companies, and a dispute over who ran the red light. A semi-truck injury claim looks similar on the surface, since both begin with a crash and an injured person, but the two are built on very different legal, regulatory, and financial foundations. Understanding those differences can shape how quickly you act, what evidence you protect, and how realistically you evaluate a settlement offer. This article compares the two types of claims across the areas that matter most: the severity of harm, the number of responsible parties, the rules that apply, insurance coverage, evidence, timelines, and damages. It is educational information, not legal advice, and state laws vary. 1. The Physical Reality: Severity of Harm The first difference is simple physics. A fully loaded tractor-trailer can weigh many times more than a passenger car. IIHS notes that large trucks often weigh 20 to 30 times as much as passenger vehicles, and that their height and ground clearance can allow lower vehicles to slide underneath a trailer. In 2024, 5,340 people died in large truck crashes, and about 62% of those killed were occupants of cars and other passenger vehicles, compared with 17% who were truck occupants. That imbalance affects claims in practical ways. Injuries in car-versus-car collisions range from minor soft-tissue strain to serious trauma, but truck crashes skew heavily toward catastrophic outcomes such as spinal cord injuries, traumatic brain injuries, amputations, and death. A claim involving lifelong care needs is valued, negotiated, and litigated very differently from one involving a few months of physical therapy. Higher stakes tend to mean more scrutiny from insurers, more experts, and more resistance. 2. Number of Potentially Liable Parties

In a typical auto collision, liability usually centers on one or two drivers. Sometimes an employer, vehicle owner, or a government entity is involved, but the analysis is comparatively contained. A semi-truck claim often involves a web of parties, including: ● The driver, for conduct such as speeding, fatigue, or distraction. ● The motor carrier, both as the employer and for its own actions, such as negligent hiring, poor training, or pressure to meet unrealistic delivery schedules. ● The freight broker that arranged the load. ● The shipper or loader, if cargo was improperly secured or overweight. ● Maintenance providers and parts manufacturers, if a mechanical failure played a role. The broker question changed significantly in 2026. On May 14, the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously held in Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, LLC that state-law negligent hiring claims against freight brokers are not preempted by the FAAAA, because they fall under its safety exception. Commentary on the ruling stresses that the standard is ordinary care and that brokers who act in good faith with reputable carriers can still defend themselves. Even so, it broadened the list of defendants in many cases, something that rarely applies to an ordinary car crash. 3. Different Rulebooks: Traffic Law vs. Federal Safety Regulation Both types of drivers must obey state traffic laws. But commercial truck drivers and carriers are also subject to the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations, covering hours of service, driver qualification, drug and alcohol testing, vehicle inspection and maintenance, and cargo securement. This matters because regulatory violations can serve as powerful evidence. If a driver exceeded federal driving-time limits or a carrier skipped required maintenance, that failure may support arguments of negligence or even recklessness. In a car accident claim, by contrast, an ordinary driver has no logbook, no driver qualification file, and no federal compliance history to examine. The truck claim therefore has an additional layer of investigation focused on the company's conduct, not just the driver's moment-to-moment decisions. 4. Insurance: A Very Different Coverage Landscape Auto collision coverage Nearly every state, with New Hampshire the notable exception, requires drivers to carry minimum liability insurance. The most common minimum is 25/50/25, meaning $25,000 per injured person, $50,000 per accident, and $25,000 for property damage. The range is wide,

from as low as 15/30/5 in Pennsylvania to 50/100/50 in North Carolina, and some states have been raising their minimums, with New Jersey moving to 35/70/25 on January 1, 2026. Those numbers can be modest compared with the cost of a serious injury. If damages exceed a policy's limits, the at-fault driver may be personally responsible for the rest unless an umbrella policy applies. Many claims therefore hinge on whether the injured person has their own uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, which about 22 states require in some form. Semi-truck coverage For-hire property carriers face a federal minimum of $750,000, and freight brokers must maintain a $75,000 surety bond. Many carriers carry more, and larger fleets often add excess or umbrella layers. Still, the federal minimum is a floor. A February 2026 FMCSA report acknowledged that current minimum levels were set decades ago and that in severe or fatal crashes, resulting costs can exceed them. The structural difference is that a truck claim often involves multiple policies, such as the carrier's primary liability policy, possible excess coverage, and a broker's coverage, and each may have its own adjuster, defense counsel, and strategy. That layered structure can increase potential recovery, but it also makes negotiations more complicated. 5. Evidence: Ordinary Records vs. Corporate Data Car accident evidence typically includes the police report, photographs, witness statements, medical records, repair estimates, and sometimes vehicle event data or nearby camera footage. It is important, but it is usually accessible and limited in volume. Truck accident evidence is broader and time-sensitive. It may include: ● Electronic logging device records showing driving and rest periods. ● Event data recorder information on speed, braking, and throttle. ● Dashcam and telematics data. ● Driver qualification and training files. ● Inspection and maintenance history. ● Dispatch records, delivery schedules, and bills of lading. The clock matters here. Federal rules require motor carriers to retain records of duty status and supporting documents for six months. Once that window passes, records may be routinely discarded unless someone formally demands their preservation. This is why attorneys in trucking cases often send preservation letters quickly, while most car accident claims do not involve a comparable rush to lock down corporate records. 6. The Adjuster and Defense Approach Insurers in both types of claims aim to limit payouts, but the resources differ. In many serious trucking cases, the carrier and its insurer mobilize investigators, accident reconstruction

professionals, and defense lawyers quickly, sometimes within hours of a crash. That rapid response can shape the record of what happened before the injured person has even left the hospital. In an everyday auto claim, the process is usually slower and more routine: an adjuster gathers statements, reviews medical bills, and negotiates. This is a general pattern rather than a rule, but injured people should recognize that a trucking defense is often better resourced and more coordinated. Giving recorded statements or signing releases without guidance can be riskier in this environment. 7. Legal Theories Unique to Trucking Claims Auto cases are most often built on ordinary negligence. Trucking cases can involve additional theories: ● Vicarious liability (respondeat superior): the company answers for its employee's negligence. ● Direct negligence: negligent hiring, retention, training, supervision, or maintenance. ● Broker negligence: careless selection of a carrier, now more clearly supported after Montgomery. ● Product liability: defective components such as tires or brakes. ● Punitive damages (in some states): for conduct like falsifying logs or knowingly ignoring safety defects. Independent-contractor arrangements can also complicate trucking cases. A driver's contract label does not always end the inquiry, since federal leasing rules and state law can still place responsibility on the carrier operating under its authority. Nothing similar arises in most private car collisions. 8. Damages and Case Valuation Both claim types can include medical expenses, lost wages, pain and suffering, and property damage. The difference is scale and complexity. Truck claims more often involve: ● Multiple surgeries and long-term rehabilitation. ● Life-care planning for permanent disability. ● Loss of earning capacity across decades. ● Wrongful death damages for families. Because these figures are larger, valuation frequently requires medical specialists, vocational experts, and economists. In routine auto claims, documentation from treating providers and a clear demand package may be enough. Higher numbers also mean insurers examine causation and pre-existing conditions more aggressively. 9. No-Fault Rules and Injury Thresholds

About a dozen states use mandatory no-fault systems, where personal injury protection coverage pays your own medical expenses and some lost income regardless of who caused the crash. This applies to occupants of passenger vehicles hit by trucks as well as to two-car crashes. The important nuance is that these states often limit lawsuits unless injuries meet a defined threshold. Truck crashes more frequently produce injuries that clear those thresholds, which can open the door to claims against the truck driver and company beyond no-fault benefits. The details depend heavily on the state. 10. Timeline and Complexity Simple auto claims can settle in a matter of months once treatment ends. Trucking claims tend to take longer because of the investigation, multiple defendants, extensive discovery, expert reports, and larger sums at stake. Cases involving interstate carriers may also raise questions about which court has jurisdiction, which can add procedural steps. Statutes of limitations apply to both, and they commonly range from one to three years for personal injury depending on the state, with different rules for wrongful death and claims against government entities. Whether the case involves a car or a semi, missing the deadline can end the claim. What Injured People Should Do Differently After a Truck Crash Some steps apply to any crash: get medical care, document the scene, keep records, and be careful about what you tell insurers. After a truck crash, a few extra steps deserve emphasis: 1. Record the truck's identifying details, including the company name, USDOT number, and license plate. 2. Act quickly on evidence. Ask an attorney about preservation letters covering logs, black box data, and maintenance files. 3. Do not assume the driver is the only responsible party. Investigate the carrier, broker, and others. 4. Be cautious with early settlement offers. Catastrophic injuries often reveal their full cost over time. 5. Check your own coverage. Underinsured motorist or medical payments coverage may matter if available limits fall short. Conclusion Auto collision claims and semi-truck injury claims share a foundation in negligence law, but they diverge sharply in practice. Truck crashes involve heavier vehicles, more severe injuries, more defendants, a federal safety regulatory overlay, different insurance structures, and much more evidence controlled by corporations. Those differences make truck claims more complex, but they also create additional avenues for accountability. Whether you are dealing with a fender bender or a life-altering collision with a tractor-trailer, understanding what makes your claim unique is the first step toward protecting your rights. Frequently Asked Questions 1. What is the biggest difference between a car accident claim and a semi-truck injury claim? The biggest difference is the scale and structure of the case. Truck crashes tend to cause more severe injuries, and IIHS data shows that most people killed in large truck crashes are occupants of passenger vehicles rather than the truck itself. Beyond severity, a truck claim usually involves more potentially responsible parties, federal safety regulations, larger insurance policies, and much more corporate evidence than an ordinary two-car collision. 2. Why do semi-truck claims often involve more than one defendant? A commercial truck crash can involve decisions made by several companies, not just the driver. The trucking company may be responsible for hiring, training, scheduling, or maintenance failures, while a shipper may have loaded the cargo unsafely, and a repair

shop or parts maker may be linked to a mechanical defect. Freight brokers are also a bigger part of the picture now. In Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, LLC, the Supreme Court held that state-law negligent hiring claims against brokers are not preempted by federal law, though brokers can still defend themselves by showing they exercised reasonable care. 3. Is the insurance coverage different for trucks compared to regular cars? Yes. Most states set car insurance minimums that are fairly modest, with 25/50/25 being the most common structure, while for-hire trucking companies must carry a federal minimum of $750,000. Larger carriers often hold additional excess coverage as well. That said, both figures are minimums, and serious injuries can exceed them, so it is important to identify every applicable policy, including your own underinsured motorist coverage if you have it. 4. Why is evidence preservation more urgent in trucking cases? Much of the key evidence in a truck case is held by the trucking company and can be lost through routine record-keeping. Federal rules require carriers to keep drivers' records of duty status and supporting documents for six months, and after that window, data may be discarded unless someone has demanded that it be preserved. Logs, black box data, dashcam footage, and maintenance records can show fatigue, speeding, or neglected repairs, which is why attorneys often send preservation letters early in truck cases. 5. Do truck accident claims take longer to resolve than car accident claims? Often, yes. A straightforward car accident claim may settle within a few months after treatment ends, but truck claims tend to move more slowly because of the investigation required, the number of parties involved, expert analysis, and the higher value of the damages. Each case is different, though, and some truck claims resolve before a lawsuit while some car claims become drawn out. Regardless of vehicle type, the statute of limitations still applies, so it is wise to confirm your state's filing deadline early.
