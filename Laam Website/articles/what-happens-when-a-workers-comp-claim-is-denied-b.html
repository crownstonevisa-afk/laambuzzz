What Happens When a Workers' Comp Claim Is Denied by Insurance

You were hurt on the job, you reported it, and you filled out the forms. Then a letter arrives from the insurance company saying your workers' compensation claim has been denied. For many injured workers, that moment brings a wave of panic: Who pays for the doctor? How will rent get paid? Did I do something wrong? A denial feels final, but it usually is not. In most states, a denial is the insurer's first position, not the last word. It opens a dispute process in which you can present evidence, challenge the insurer's reasoning, and ask a judge or board to decide. This article explains what a denial actually means, the most common reasons claims are denied, exactly what to do in the days and weeks that follow, how appeals work, and what mistakes to avoid. It is general information, not legal advice, and workers' compensation procedures vary considerably by state. First, Keep the Numbers in Perspective Workplace injuries are common. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that private industry employers recorded about 2.5 million nonfatal workplace injury and illness cases in 2024. With that many injuries, disputes between workers and insurers are inevitable. A denial does not mean you were dishonest or that your injury is not real. Often it reflects paperwork problems, disagreements about causation, or an insurer's decision to contest a claim that it believes carries risk. What "Denied" Actually Means When an insurer denies a claim, it is stating that it believes your injury is not covered by workers' compensation. But denials come in several forms, and it is important to know which one you are facing: ● Full claim denial. The insurer rejects the entire claim, so no medical care or wage benefits are paid. ● Partial denial. The insurer accepts the claim for some injuries or body parts but denies others, such as accepting a shoulder injury but rejecting a related neck problem. ● Denial of specific treatment. The claim is accepted, but a particular surgery, medication, or therapy is refused, often after a medical review. ● Termination or suspension of benefits. Payments already in progress are stopped, sometimes because the insurer says you have recovered, can return to work, or missed an appointment.

● Dispute over disability level. The insurer accepts the claim but disagrees about how much impairment you have or how long benefits should last. The appeal strategy for each type differs, so read your notice carefully. Why Workers' Comp Claims Are Denied Insurers must generally provide a reason for denial. Common grounds include: Reason for Denial What It Usually Means How Workers Often Respond Late reporting or filing Notice to the employer or the claim form came after the deadline Show the employer knew earlier, or that an exception applies Injury not work-related Insurer claims the injury happened outside work or is unrelated to job duties Provide witness accounts, incident reports, and medical opinions linking the injury to work Weak medical documentation Records do not clearly connect the condition to the job Ask your treating doctor for a detailed report on causation Unfavorable independent medical exam An insurer-selected doctor disagreed with your doctor Challenge the findings with treating-physician records and testimony Pre-existing condition Insurer says the problem existed before work Show that work aggravated or accelerated the condition Employment status dispute Employer claims you were an independent contractor Present evidence of control, pay structure, and how the work was done Outside the scope of employment Injury allegedly occurred during a personal activity or non-work errand Document the work purpose of the activity Policy violations or misconduct Allegations of intoxication, horseplay, or safety-rule violations Contest factual accusations and legal defenses Coverage problems Employer allegedly had no insurance or a lapse in coverage Check for uninsured-employer funds or other remedies Alleged inconsistencies Insurer points to conflicting statements Clarify and correct the record with consistent documentation

One summary of denial causes lists missed reporting deadlines, disputes over whether the injury is work-related, thin medical documentation, and unfavorable independent medical exams as leading examples. Understanding which category applies to you is the first step in fixing the problem. Denial Versus Delay Not every slow response is a denial. In California, for example, after a claim form is filed the insurer generally has 90 days to accept or deny the claim, and if it does not reject liability within that period the injury is presumed compensable. During that investigation period, up to $10,000 in medical treatment is generally authorized. Other states have their own timelines for how quickly an insurer must respond and may require the carrier to explain in writing why it is disputing benefits. If you are waiting on a decision, keep documenting your injury, keep receiving medical care, and keep proof of when your claim form was delivered, because those dates often matter. What to Do Immediately After a Denial Step 1: Read the denial letter carefully The letter should state the specific reason for the denial and how to challenge it. State law typically requires that denial notices include the deadline for filing an appeal. Note the date on the letter, because appeal clocks often begin running from the date it was issued or received, not from the day you happened to open it. Step 2: Calendar the deadline right away Appeal deadlines are usually short. One commonly cited range is 30 to 90 days from the denial, and some are shorter. In Maryland, for instance, a worker generally has 30 days from receiving the denial letter to file an appeal. Deadlines can also vary by the type of denial, such as an initial claim denial versus a termination of ongoing benefits. Guidance for injured workers emphasizes that a missed filing deadline usually cannot be extended, although you can often add evidence later. When in doubt, file something in writing before the deadline, even a simple form, and supplement the record afterward. Step 3: Look for a simple fix Sometimes the problem is a clerical error, a missing form, or a misunderstanding. Before launching a full dispute, it may help to speak with your employer and the adjuster to find out exactly what the insurer needs. FindLaw's guidance on denied claims notes that a denial can stem from a simple error or misunderstanding that may be resolved without a full appeal. Keep these communications in writing when possible. Do not let informal talks delay your appeal deadline. Step 4: Request your claim file

Ask the insurer for a complete copy of your claim file, including the adjuster's notes, medical reviews, any independent medical examination report, recorded statements, and surveillance if any exists. Knowing what the insurer relied on lets you respond precisely. Step 5: Strengthen your medical evidence Medical proof is often the deciding factor. Ask your treating doctor to write a clear report explaining: ● The diagnosis. ● How and why the injury or condition relates to your job duties or the workplace incident. ● Your work restrictions and expected recovery. ● If applicable, why the doctor disagrees with the insurer's medical examiner. Guidance for denied claimants points out that an independent medical examiner is chosen and paid by the insurer, so its conclusions can be challenged with your own treating doctor's records and testimony at a hearing. Consistent records from the start, including the initial description of how the injury happened, carry weight. Step 6: File your appeal or request for a hearing Appeals go through your state's workers' compensation agency, board, or commission. Depending on the state, the process may involve an application for hearing, a conference or mediation, and a formal hearing. If the board or commission rules against you, further review can often be sought in an appellate court. In some states, the process has multiple steps and forms, each with its own deadline. Step 7: Keep receiving medical care Do not stop treatment just because the claim was denied. Your health matters, and gaps in care can also be used to argue your injury is not serious. Options for paying for care while the dispute is pending may include your own health insurance (with the understanding that it may seek reimbursement later), community health resources, or providers willing to treat under a lien if state rules allow. Keep every bill and receipt. Step 8: Consider consulting an attorney Workers' compensation lawyers commonly handle denied claims and typically work on contingency, with fees regulated by state law in many places. The insurance company's lawyers deal with this process every day. Legal help can be particularly valuable when the denial involves medical causation disputes, employment status, or complex deadlines. What Happens at a Workers' Comp Hearing? If your dispute is not resolved informally, it may be heard by an administrative law judge or workers' compensation judge. California's Division of Workers' Compensation, for example,

notes that contested cases are heard by an administrative judge at local offices. New York's process similarly includes a pre-hearing conference and a formal hearing before an administrative law judge. Typical hearing steps include: 1. Pre-hearing conference or mediation, where the judge or mediator identifies disputed issues and may encourage settlement. 2. Exchange of evidence, including medical reports and records. 3. Testimony, from you, and sometimes from witnesses, supervisors, or doctors. 4. Argument, where each side explains why the evidence supports its position. 5. A written decision, which may award benefits, uphold the denial, or resolve only certain issues. Hearings are less formal than civil trials, but preparation still matters. Consistency between your testimony, your medical records, and your earlier statements is crucial. How Long Does the Process Take? There is no single answer. Some disputes are resolved within weeks through corrections or negotiation, while litigated cases can run many months or even years. One California-focused source estimates that contested cases can last two to four years from injury to final resolution, though this varies widely. Timelines depend on the state, the complexity of the medical issues, and how crowded the hearing calendar is. Can a Denied Claim Still Be Won? Yes, and denials are frequently overturned or compromised through settlement when the worker has solid evidence. However, outcomes are never guaranteed, and statistics circulating online about success rates are often unreliable and differ by state. What is consistent is that claims with clear timely reporting, consistent statements, strong medical opinions, and witness support tend to be in a better position than those built on gaps and contradictions. Special Situations Denial for late reporting If notice came after your state's deadline, the insurer may deny the claim. Exceptions sometimes apply, such as when the employer actually knew about the injury, when the delay was due to a gradual condition you did not immediately connect to work, or when the employer misled you. Gather proof that anyone in management knew of the injury. Denial based on a pre-existing condition

Having a prior condition does not necessarily bar a claim. If work aggravated, accelerated, or combined with an earlier problem to cause disability or the need for treatment, many systems still provide coverage, though the rules differ. Be honest about your history, since concealment damages credibility. Denial because you are considered an independent contractor Employment classification is a common battleground, particularly in delivery, trucking, and gig work. Whether you are an employee depends on factors such as who controls how the work is done, who supplies tools and vehicles, and how you are paid. Contract labels are not always decisive. Denial of a specific treatment If the insurer refuses a recommended surgery or medication, most states have a medical dispute or review process, sometimes with strict deadlines and independent reviewers. Ask your doctor to document why the treatment is necessary. Termination of ongoing benefits If payments suddenly stop, ask for the reason in writing and check the appeal time limits immediately. Insurers sometimes rely on an examination or surveillance to argue that you can work. Options Beyond Workers' Comp A denial in the workers' comp system does not necessarily close every door. ● Third-party claims. If someone other than your employer contributed to your injury, such as a negligent driver, a manufacturer of defective equipment, or another contractor, you may have a separate personal injury claim. That claim runs independently and can seek damages workers' comp does not cover, such as pain and suffering. It has its own deadline, often one to three years but varying by state. ● Short-term or long-term disability insurance, if you have coverage through work or privately. ● Health insurance, for medical treatment, recognizing that reimbursement rules may apply. ● Social Security disability benefits, for severe, long-lasting impairments, which involve a separate application and eligibility rules. ● Unemployment benefits, in limited situations, depending on state rules and your ability to work. Protection Against Retaliation Some workers worry that appealing a denial will cost them their job. Federal law prohibits retaliation for reporting a work-related injury, and OSHA has explained that employers cannot take adverse action that would discourage a reasonable employee from reporting an

injury. A complaint under Section 11(c) of the OSH Act generally must be filed within 30 days of the retaliation, and many states have additional protections. If you notice sudden discipline, schedule changes, or termination after reporting an injury or filing a claim, document it and seek advice promptly. Common Mistakes After a Denial ● Missing the appeal deadline. This is the most damaging error. ● Ignoring the letter or assuming it is final. ● Stopping medical treatment. It weakens your case and harms your recovery. ● Giving inconsistent accounts to doctors, adjusters, and your employer. ● Discussing the case on social media. Insurers may review public posts, and photographs or comments can be taken out of context. ● Exaggerating symptoms or hiding prior injuries. Credibility is central, and fraud allegations carry serious consequences. ● Signing releases or settlement documents without understanding them. ● Going it alone in complex disputes, especially those involving medical causation or employment status. A Practical Checklist 1. Read the denial letter and identify the exact reason. 2. Write down the appeal deadline and confirm it with your state agency. 3. Keep all letters, envelopes, emails, and notes of phone calls. 4. Request your claim file and any medical review reports. 5. Ask your doctor for a detailed causation and restrictions report. 6. Collect witness statements and incident records. 7. File your appeal or hearing request on time. 8. Continue treatment and track expenses and lost income. 9. Consider a consultation with a workers' compensation attorney. 10. Explore whether a third party may be responsible. Conclusion A workers' compensation denial is a setback, not necessarily the end of your claim. Understanding why the insurer said no, acting quickly within the appeal deadline, building strong medical and factual evidence, and using your state's dispute process can change the outcome. Because the rules, timelines, and forms differ from state to state, getting advice specific to your situation can make the difference between an overlooked right and a successful appeal. Frequently Asked Questions 1. What should I do first if my workers' comp claim is denied?

Read the denial letter carefully to understand the stated reason, and note the appeal deadline immediately, since it often starts on the date of the letter. Then request your claim file, gather medical records and witness information, and file an appeal or hearing request with your state's workers' compensation agency before the deadline. Even if you are still collecting evidence, filing something in writing on time is usually safer than waiting. 2. How long do I have to appeal a denied workers' comp claim? It depends on your state and the type of denial. Deadlines are commonly in the range of 30 to 90 days, and some are shorter. For example, Maryland generally gives 30 days from receipt of the denial letter. Because a missed deadline usually cannot be extended, confirm the exact time limit with your state agency or an attorney as soon as you get the notice. 3. Can I still get medical treatment while my claim is being disputed? Sometimes. Some states require insurers to authorize limited treatment during the investigation period; California, for example, generally authorizes up to $10,000 in medical care while a claim is being investigated. Once a claim is formally denied, you may need to rely on health insurance or other resources while you appeal, and keep all medical records and bills. Continuing treatment is important both for your recovery and for supporting your case. 4. Does hiring a lawyer help with a denied claim? Often it does, especially in cases involving disputed medical causation, independent medical exams, employment status questions, or complex deadlines. Workers' compensation attorneys commonly work on a contingency basis, with fees regulated in many states, and many offer free initial consultations. An attorney can help gather evidence, prepare you for a hearing, and negotiate with the insurer's representatives. 5. Can I file a lawsuit if my workers' comp claim is denied? Usually you cannot sue your employer for negligence because workers' compensation is generally the exclusive remedy, and a denial typically leads to an appeal within the workers' comp system instead. However, if a third party, such as a negligent driver, an equipment manufacturer, or another contractor, contributed to your injury, you may have a separate personal injury lawsuit that runs alongside or independently of the workers' comp dispute.
